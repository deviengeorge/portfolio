Every semester, the Faculty of Science exam office builds a final-exam timetable by hand. It took about **four weeks**. My graduation project, Nassaq (نسق), does it in **under three minutes**, and every hard rule is checked before the result is saved. The project was rated Excellent, and the office uses it as v1.0.

This is how it works and what I learned. The source is private, so I describe the design and show the app with synthetic data.

## Why a timetable is hard to do by hand

A timetable looks like a list of dates. It is really a pile of rules that fight each other:

- A student can never sit two exams at the same time.
- Every enrolled student needs a seat, and a big course may need several rooms in the same period.
- Every occupied room needs invigilators, and one invigilator can cover only one room per period.
- Students should get rest between exams, and no one should have three exams in a day if it can be avoided.

Change one exam and you have to re-check all of it, plus the seating lists and the printed reports. That re-checking is where the four weeks went.

## Hard rules and preferences are different things

The most useful decision was separating the two kinds of rule.

**Hard rules** must hold or the timetable is invalid: no student clashes, enough seats, rooms and invigilators available. **Preferences** are goals: more rest between exams, fewer exams per day, mornings, fewer buildings per course.

Preferences get penalties that grow with every extra exam on a day, so three exams in one day costs more than two days of two exams each. Priorities are ordered, so improving a lower priority can never make a higher one worse.

## Three stages instead of one giant model

Solving everything in one model is slow and hard to debug. Nassaq runs three stages with OR-Tools CP-SAT:

1. **Course to period**: choose a time slot for every course.
2. **Room allocation**: for each period, assign rooms and split large exams across rooms.
3. **Invigilators**: assign people, then balance their duties.

If room allocation proves a period impossible, the search goes back to stage one. A timeout is *not* treated as proof of impossibility, because "ran out of time" and "no solution exists" are different statements. The app says so instead of pretending.

## Never trust the solver alone

After solving, a separate validator re-checks the full result against every enabled hard rule. Only a result that passes is saved. The solver and the validator are written independently, so one bug in the model can't silently produce a bad timetable.

## Late changes without starting over

Exams move after the timetable is published. Adjustment mode takes the existing timetable as the starting point and keeps previous periods and rooms wherever the hard rules allow. The office changes what it needs to change, not everything.

## Built for the people who use it

- Arabic, right-to-left interface in React, wrapped as a Windows desktop app with Tauri.
- Imports the university's registration spreadsheets (including the xls export from the registration system) and exports weekly views, per-person PDFs, and Excel files.
- Works offline. Student data stays on the office's own machine.

## What I would tell someone building something similar

1. **Write the rules down first.** The rules document became the spec, the tests, and the help text.
2. **Separate "must" from "prefer".** It makes the model smaller and the results explainable.
3. **Validate independently.** A checker that shares no code with the solver is worth the extra day.
4. **Be honest about limits.** The app never claims an optimal answer, and the office trusts it more for that.
5. **Test with the real person's workflow.** Upload, check, rules, generate, review, export. Anything outside that path is a distraction.
